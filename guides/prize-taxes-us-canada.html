Do You Pay Tax on Prizes? US and Canada Explained

How prize winnings are treated for tax in the United States and Canada, what fair market value means, and how to plan for a big win.

By the WinFast.com Editorial Team · Reviewed against official sources · Editorial policy

Winning a car, a trip or a cash prize feels free — until tax season. The rules are very different on each side of the border.

United States: prizes are income

The IRS treats prizes and awards from sweepstakes, contests, raffles and lotteries as taxable income. Non-cash prizes count at fair market value (FMV) — what the item would sell for, which may be lower than the advertised ARV. Winnings are reported on your Form 1040 (Schedule 1), even if you never receive a tax form.

  • Sponsors commonly report larger prizes to the IRS on Form 1099-MISC; gambling-type winnings may come with Form W-2G. Check the current IRS instructions for reporting thresholds, which can change.
  • Most states with an income tax also tax prize income.
  • Large wins can push you into a higher bracket and may require estimated tax payments.
Strategy: for big non-cash prizes, check the rules for a cash alternative, set aside funds immediately, and talk to a tax professional before accepting. You can usually decline a prize you can't afford the tax on.

Canada: windfalls are generally tax-free

The Canada Revenue Agency generally does not tax lottery winnings or prizes won by chance as an individual — they are treated as windfalls. Exceptions include prizes connected to your employment or business. However, income earned on winnings (interest, dividends, capital gains) is taxable.

Elsewhere

Rules vary widely: some countries tax prizes at source, others not at all. If you enter international promotions, check the rules and your local tax authority.
